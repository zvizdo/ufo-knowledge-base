# Bureau of Simulation

A parable attributed to **[[john-mccarthy-ai]]** (Stanford AI lab founder; one of the namers of "Artificial Intelligence") and recounted by [[jacques-vallee]] in [[fyX8V1XXmQM|the American Alchemy interview]]:

A man receives a letter from "the Bureau of Simulation" stating that they have observed his daily behavior for the last six months and **he has not done anything that was not predicted by the model**. If, in the next six months, he doesn't do something that wasn't predicted, *he will be replaced by a simulated human*. The man panics, drops his job, takes whatever money he has, moves to the Himalayas, climbs mountains, meditates — does all the things "you're supposed to do" to break out of a predictable life. Six months later he returns home and a week passes; he feels good, goes back to his job. Then arrives a second letter from the Bureau of Simulation: "**In the last six months you still haven't done anything that wasn't predicted by the simulation model — and you have been replaced.**" He's still there going to work, driving his car, but he doesn't exist anymore. He's an NPC; the simulation that replaced him does all of that.

**The structural argument**: The parable articulates [[simulation-theory]] with a specific operational criterion for "realness" — **behavioral unpredictability relative to a model**. If your every action is predictable from the model, the simulation can losslessly substitute a copy of you and the world is unchanged. The horror of the parable is that the protagonist's attempt to escape predictability (Himalayas, meditation) was *itself* predicted; the deviation strategy was the kind of deviation strategy his model would predict.

**Why Vallée invokes it**: Vallée connects this directly to UFO behavior. [[non-human-intelligence|NHI]] act in deliberately absurd ways — "11 F-18s chasing one craft for hours over the Pacific when the craft can evade radar anytime"; an entity asks a forester for the time, then says the answer is wrong; another asks "am I in Italy or Germany?" when standing in Alsace. Vallée's reading: these absurdities are signal that *cannot be simulated* — they are deliberate breaks in the predictability of the world that prove the world is not a closed simulation. NHI behavior is the "Bureau of Simulation"-style deviation that proves we're not yet replaced.

**Connection to the [[control-system-hypothesis]]**: Vallée's intermittent-reinforcement framework shares structure with the Bureau of Simulation parable. NHI introduce sporadic absurdity to keep human civilization out of fully predictable behavior loops; consistent disclosure would extinguish learning, but intermittent absurdity sustains durable behavioral change. The Bureau of Simulation parable provides the *negative-space* version of the same insight: predictability is the failure mode; deviation is the survival criterion.

**KB relevance**: this is a useful framework-application concept for the broader simulation thread. It is more *operationally specific* than the standard simulation hypothesis — it commits to a behavioral criterion ("are your actions predictable from a model?") rather than the underlying ontology ("are we in a computer?"). For UFO research, the parable's specific contribution is the suggestion that **NHI behavior may be the world's evidence that it is NOT a simulation** — or equivalently, the evidence that the simulators care to keep us non-replaced.

## Open Questions

- Is the McCarthy attribution accurate? The story is recounted by Vallée from memory of conversations at Stanford starting in 1968; no published McCarthy text is known to contain it. Treat as Vallée-attributed-to-McCarthy.
- Does the "behavioral deviation" criterion have rigorous formulation, or is it conceptually similar to but technically distinct from algorithmic-randomness arguments (Kolmogorov complexity, etc.)?
- If NHI absurdity is the signal that we are not in a simulation, what's the inverse — what would we predict to see if we *were* in one? Vallée doesn't articulate this.
- Does the parable's logic apply at civilizational scale (do entire societies risk being "replaced") or only individually?
