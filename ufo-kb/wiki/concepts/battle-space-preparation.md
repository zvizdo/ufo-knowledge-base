# Battle-Space Preparation

The hypothesis, articulated by [[lue-elizondo]] during his time at [[aatip]], that UAP behavior around military platforms and installations may represent systematic reconnaissance — documenting the performance envelope of U.S. fighter aircraft, the geometry of carrier strike group defenses, and the reaction time of radar and missile systems. In military parlance, "battle-space preparation" describes the process of mapping an adversary's capabilities, response protocols, and vulnerabilities before initiating combat operations.

The hypothesis arose from pattern analysis: UAP are disproportionately reported near nuclear weapons storage, carrier strike groups, submarine bases, and test ranges. They frequently approach military platforms in sequences that appear to test response — triggering radar, approaching aircraft, then departing when intercepted. The [[2004-nimitz-tic-tac]] incident and the [[2014-east-coast-uap]] show UAP tracking carrier operations and demonstrating performance capabilities (instant acceleration, no observable propulsion) that would be tactically relevant intelligence about the adversary's limitations.

The battle-space preparation interpretation was initially Elizondo's working assumption; he later described it as his "least bad" explanation rather than a conclusion. It competes with the NHI observation hypothesis (passive monitoring of military development) and the non-adversarial intelligence model (mapping for navigational or environmental reasons).

Key implication: if UAP are conducting battle-space preparation, then the operational response is different from if they are neutral observers — it would warrant active countermeasures, not just documentation.
