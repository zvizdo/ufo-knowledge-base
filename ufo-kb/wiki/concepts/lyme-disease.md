# Lyme Disease

Bacterial illness caused by Borrelia burgdorferi, transmitted primarily through deer tick bites. First clinically described in Lyme, Connecticut in 1975. One of the most common vector-borne diseases in North America, characterized by a distinctive bull's-eye rash, flu-like symptoms, and in untreated cases, multi-system complications including neurological and cardiac involvement.

In the KB (via the [[plum-island]] page and related material), Lyme disease appears in connection with [[plum-island|Plum Island Animal Disease Center]] — a federally operated biological research facility located on an island in Long Island Sound, approximately 10 miles from Old Lyme, Connecticut, where the disease was first identified. The proximity has fueled a persistent hypothesis that Lyme disease originated from or was accidentally (or deliberately) released by biological research conducted at Plum Island.

The Plum Island connection to Lyme disease has been documented by journalists (notably Michael Carroll in *Lab 257*, 2004) and is not officially confirmed by government agencies. In the KB's framing, it is treated as structurally analogous to the [[gain-of-function-research]] and COVID lab-leak cluster: dual-use biological research conducted in a government facility with inadequate containment, adjacent to a major civilian population center.

**The *Lab 257* thesis**: Carroll's investigation found that Plum Island conducted research on tick-borne pathogens (including studies involving ticks as potential biological warfare vectors) during the Cold War era. The facility's tick-containment protocols were inadequate, and Lyme disease first appeared in the communities closest to the island. The "10 miles" proximity is geographical coincidence on its face; Carroll's argument is that the specific pathogen profile, timing of emergence, and directional spread from the Plum Island–proximate shoreline is more consistent with accidental laboratory release than wild emergence.

**Status**: The Plum Island–Lyme disease hypothesis is not established by peer-reviewed evidence, and government agencies uniformly deny any connection. It remains in the same evidential category as other government-research-facility disease origin hypotheses: plausible mechanism, geographic correlation, institutional motive for concealment, and no smoking-gun documentation. The KB treats it as a live hypothesis in the [[deep-state-continuity]] pattern.
