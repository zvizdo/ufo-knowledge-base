# Many-Worlds Interpretation

The Many-Worlds Interpretation (MWI) of quantum mechanics, proposed by [[hugh-everett]] (Hugh Everett III) in his 1957 Princeton dissertation, holds that the quantum mechanical wavefunction never collapses. Instead, every quantum measurement causes the universe to branch — all possible outcomes occur simultaneously in separate, non-interacting branches of reality. There is no privileged "observed" outcome; all outcomes are equally real in their respective branches.

**Physics basis**: Standard quantum mechanics requires a "measurement problem" — ordinary physical evolution is deterministic and continuous (Schrödinger equation), but measurement produces a single definite outcome, seemingly requiring discontinuous collapse. The MWI eliminates collapse entirely: the observer also branches, with one copy of the observer perceiving each outcome. The appearance of a single outcome is a feature of the observer's local branch, not of reality as a whole.

**Hugh Everett**: Everett developed the MWI at Princeton under John Wheeler's supervision. After his dissertation was largely ignored by the physics community, he left academia and spent his career in defense work at the Pentagon and as a defense contractor. The interpretation was revived and popularized by Bryce DeWitt in the 1970s.

**Andreessen / Willow statement**: [[mark-andreessen]], co-founder of [[andreessen-horowitz]], publicly stated in 2024 that Google's Willow quantum chip had "proven" the multiverse, citing the chip's quantum computational performance as evidence that parallel universes must be doing computational work. The statement attracted both interest and physicist pushback, but its significance in the KB lies in a Silicon Valley technology investor publicly asserting multiverse reality — and a subsequent meeting with the National Security Council on "classified physics."

**UAP connection**: The MWI and related multiverse frameworks appear in UAP discourse primarily through the [[interdimensional-hypothesis]] — the proposal that NHI originate from or transit through parallel branches of reality rather than distant points in three-dimensional space.
