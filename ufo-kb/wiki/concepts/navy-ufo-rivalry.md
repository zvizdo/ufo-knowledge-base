# Navy–Air Force UFO Rivalry

The Navy–Air Force rivalry over UFO investigation authority is one of the structural dynamics running beneath the surface of US UAP history. [[richard-dolan]] identifies it as a persistent force from the late 1940s through the present.

## Origins

The tension has roots in the 1947 National Security Act, which unified the US military under a Department of Defense structure and created the Air Force as an independent service branch. This reorganization was contested: the Navy fought to preserve its institutional independence (particularly [[james-forrestal]]'s battles over naval aviation and nuclear weapon access). The "revolt of the admirals" (~1949) is the most visible expression of this tension.

When UFOs began being officially investigated (culminating in [[project-blue-book]] under Air Force control), the Navy resented being excluded from formal investigation authority — especially since Navy ships and aircraft were having their own encounters.

## Pattern of Air Force Dominance

[[donald-keyhoe]] — a retired Marine Corps Major with deep connections to top Navy brass — documented the pattern: Air Force personnel would board Navy vessels following a UAP encounter and take over the investigation, freezing out Navy personnel. Navy officers found this jurisdictionally and professionally offensive.

## The Kimball-Radford Incident (1950)

The 1950 sighting is the defining personal episode in this rivalry. [[dan-kimball]] (Secretary of the Navy) and [[arthur-radford]] (Admiral) were flying separately from California to Hawaii when both aircraft were accosted by an unknown object that circled each plane. The encounter was credible, firsthand, witnessed by the Navy's civilian and military leadership simultaneously.

Kimball's response: order an independent Navy investigation — bypassing Air Force jurisdictional claims. The Navy was not going to let the Air Force manage its encounter data.

## NICAP and Congressional Hearing Attempt

[[donald-keyhoe]]'s work with [[nicap]] to force congressional UFO hearings (circa 1962) was partly a Navy-adjacent operation: Keyhoe's Navy connections gave him access to witnesses and encounter data that the Air Force was managing to suppress. The suspicious resignation of [[roscoe-hillenkoetter]] from NICAP at precisely the moment these hearings were being organized suggests active pressure to contain a Navy-adjacent civilian disclosure push.

## Richard Dolan's Framing

Dolan argues that the Navy's early and deep involvement in UAP creates a separate intelligence thread:

- The Office of Naval Intelligence (ONI) is one of the oldest US intelligence organizations (established 1890s) — older than the CIA
- The Navy's USO (Unidentified Submersible Object) encounter database is uniquely deep: the ocean represents a distinct domain where Navy vessels encounter phenomena the Air Force has no jurisdiction over
- [[nat-kobitz]] (Navy Director of Science & Technology, dying of cancer) disclosed seeing UAP technology at [[wright-patterson]] — demonstrating that senior Navy figures had separate access to the most classified materials
- [[james-copley]]'s account from the USS John F. Kennedy (1971 orb incident) shows the Navy's continued pattern of encountering phenomena and then suppressing internal reporting

## Modern Relevance

The Navy's unilateral release of the Nimitz/FLIR footage in 2017 (supporting the [[2017-nyt-uap-story]]) was not coordinated with Air Force or broader DoD — another expression of the institutional independence dynamic. The Navy's willingness to acknowledge its own encounter data without waiting for Air Force or CIA approval has been a recurring feature of the modern disclosure period.
