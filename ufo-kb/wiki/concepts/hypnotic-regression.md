# Hypnotic Regression

The clinical practice of inducing a hypnotic state in a subject to facilitate recovery of memories that may be inaccessible in normal waking consciousness. In the UAP and abduction research context, regression has been used extensively to recover detailed accounts from experiencers who have missing time, fragmented memories, or apparent trauma following anomalous encounters.

**Methodological case for regression**: Hypnotic states lower the subject's cognitive resistance to emotionally charged material, can access memories encoded during dissociative or traumatic states, and allow a trained therapist to take a structured history. [[leo-sprinkle]]'s axiom — "all hypnosis is self-hypnosis" — frames the method as consciousness-exploratory rather than purely memory-retrieval, acknowledging that hypnotic content reflects the subject's internal processing as much as external events.

**Methodological case against**: Hypnotic memory is significantly more susceptible to confabulation, suggestion, and false-memory implantation than ordinary recall. Subjects in hypnotic states are highly responsive to leading questions and therapist expectations. Court systems have increasingly rejected hypnotically recovered memory as evidence; memory researchers (Elizabeth Loftus et al.) have demonstrated systematic false-memory induction.

In the KB, both positions appear: [[john-mack]] used regression extensively and subjected results to cross-case analysis; crash witnesses at [[varginha]] (Carlos de Souza) underwent regression under medical supervision. The credibility of any specific regression-recovered account depends heavily on the methodology, the session record, and convergence with physical or independent evidence.
