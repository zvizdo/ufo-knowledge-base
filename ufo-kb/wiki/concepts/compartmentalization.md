# Compartmentalization

The security architecture principle by which classified programs are divided into discrete information compartments, each accessible only to personnel whose specific job function requires it ("need to know"). No individual — including program managers, oversight personnel, or even the President — necessarily has access to all compartments of a given program.

**Structure**: Above standard classification levels (Confidential/Secret/Top Secret), Sensitive Compartmented Information (SCI) and Special Access Programs (SAPs) add horizontal layers. A person might hold TS/SCI clearance but still be excluded from a given compartment if they are not "read into" that specific program. Different aspects of the same program can be in different compartments — propulsion research, materials analysis, intelligence collection, and oversight may never communicate.

**Application to UAP programs**: In the KB, compartmentalization is the mechanism through which UAP-related programs may have been maintained without oversight from Congress, the Inspector General, or senior executive officials. [[david-grusch]] describes programs where the compartmentalization was so fine-grained that individuals working adjacent to UAP-related activities were unaware of their neighbor's work. [[james-clapper]] and [[lue-elizondo]] have both described encountering UAP classification structures that exceeded their authorized access.

**Why it enables persistent secrecy**: Standard declassification review requires knowing what to review. If compartmentation is opaque — if even the program's existence is in a compartment — the 25-year automatic declassification review cycle (per EO 13526) cannot trigger. This is the structural explanation for how programs could persist through administrations without executive knowledge.

**Relationship to UAP oversight problem**: The [[uap-disclosure-act]] provisions attempt to address this by requiring program owners to certify to a new review board rather than relying on routine declassification. The structural problem compartmentalization creates — legitimate programs invisible to legitimate oversight — is the core argument for why congressional UAP legislation had to define new mandatory disclosure mechanisms rather than rely on existing oversight structures.
