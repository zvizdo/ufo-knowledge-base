# Catastrophism

The thesis that Earth's history — both geological and civilizational — has been shaped more by sudden, catastrophic events than by the gradualist processes favored by mainstream geology and archaeology. Contrasted with "uniformitarianism," the dominant scientific framework since Lyell, which holds that the same slow processes observable today have always governed Earth's development.

In the UAP/ancient-civilization KB cluster, catastrophism is most relevant through the [[younger-dryas-impact]] hypothesis: a proposed comet or asteroid impact event around 12,900 years ago that triggered abrupt climate change, mass extinctions, and the collapse of an advanced pre-Ice Age civilization. [[randall-carlson]] and [[graham-hancock]] are the central modern proponents of this framing in the KB, arguing that global flood myths, the sudden emergence of agriculture and monument-building at the Younger Dryas boundary, and anomalous geological features like the Carolina bays all point to a catastrophe that reset human civilization and erased most of its material record.

The connection to UAP is structural: if advanced human civilization was periodically wiped out, the "anomalous" technology humans demonstrate in ancient megalithic sites may represent recovery rather than invention. The catastrophism thesis also challenges the linear-progress model of human history that makes the existence of ancient advanced technology seem impossible.
