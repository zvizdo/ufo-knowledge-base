# UAP Physical Evidence

Physical evidence encompasses the subset of UAP evidence that is objectively measurable — distinct from testimonial accounts, which depend on witness credibility. Physical evidence categories include:

**Physical trace evidence (CE2)**:
Landing traces in soil and vegetation matching the apparent geometry of a landed craft; radiation readings elevated above background at landing sites; damaged or scorched vegetation; impressions consistent with mechanical landing gear. The most well-documented landing trace case is the [[1964-socorro]] (Lonnie Zamora, New Mexico).

**Witness biological effects**:
Burns, radiation syndrome symptoms, eye damage, and other injuries documented by physicians. The [[1967-falcon-lake]] grid-burn case (Michalak) and [[1980-cash-landrum-incident]] (Cash, Landrum, Landrum) are the best-documented examples in North America. Physical effects that would require actual physical cause eliminate the purely hallucination or misperception explanation.

**Sensor data**:
Multi-radar tracking of UAP; military sensor fusion data (radar + FLIR + ATFLIR + pilot visual) as in the [[2004-nimitz-tic-tac]] encounter; atmospheric data from balloon launches correlated with UAP appearances. Sensor data is significant because it is generated by systems not subject to witness psychology.

**Materials**:
Alleged recovered materials with anomalous properties — unusual isotopic ratios, unusual alloy compositions, bi-layered metallic composites. The evidentiary chain of custody for such materials is contested; no publicly authenticated sample with independently verified anomalous properties has been established. See [[metamaterials]].

**KB significance**: Physical evidence is treated as higher evidential weight than testimonial evidence in [[credibility-frameworks]], precisely because it does not depend on witness credibility or memory. The gap between the strength of available physical evidence and the government's public acknowledgment position is one of the central puzzles of UAP research.
