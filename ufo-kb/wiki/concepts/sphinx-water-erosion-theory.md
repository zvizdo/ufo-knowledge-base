# Sphinx Water Erosion Theory

## Core Argument

Developed by geologist [[robert-schoch]] and alternative Egyptologist [[john-anthony-west]]. The erosion visible on the body of the [[great-sphinx]] and the walls of its enclosure consists of deep vertical fissures and rounded profiles characteristic of **precipitation-induced erosion** — formed by heavy rainfall flowing across and into the limestone over extended periods.

This type of erosion is fundamentally different from the wind/sand erosion that dominates in Egypt's current desert climate and that marks other limestone structures at [[giza-plateau]] from the dynastic period.

For the precipitation erosion to have formed, the Sphinx must have been exposed during a period when Egypt received substantial annual rainfall — the African Humid Period (also called the Green Sahara), which ended roughly 5,000–7,000 BC. The conventional dynastic date for the Sphinx (~2500 BC, during Khafre's reign) post-dates this wet period by millennia.

## Implications

If the erosion argument is correct:
- The Sphinx's body was carved no later than ~7000 BC, possibly earlier
- This predates the First Dynasty of Egypt by at least 3,500 years
- It implies the existence of a civilization capable of carving a massive monument during the pre-dynastic period
- The same architect may be responsible for the [[osireion]] and the Valley Temple at Giza (also built in anomalous megalithic style)

## Mainstream Counterarguments

Egyptologists largely attribute the erosion to wind, salt weathering, and periodic desert flash floods. They dispute Schoch's precipitation-only interpretation and maintain the Khafre dating based on the nearby Khafre Valley Temple and stylistic analysis.

## Current Consensus in the Alternative Community

[[luke-caverns]] says this is "probably the strongest argument in all of Egypt of an exceptionally old artifact or monument." Even [[graham-hancock]] — who has revised his pyramid dating toward the 4th dynasty — accepts the Sphinx water erosion argument as pointing to pre-dynastic construction.

## Carlson's Limestone Erosion Research (JGE1NIGhBzw)

[[randall-carlson]] conducted independent limestone erosion studies to evaluate the Sphinx ditch erosion:

**Methods**:
1. **Gravestone erosion**: Dated gravestones (e.g., pre-Civil War cemetery) give known exposure dates; erosion rates derivable by measuring material loss
2. **Metal tenon studies**: Researchers drill into cliff faces in high-energy surf zones, insert metal tenons, measure how far the cliff face recedes over years/decades
3. **Smithsonian cube**: A cube of freshly cut limestone placed on a Smithsonian rooftop; monitored over decades; corner rounding and material loss quantified
4. **Luminescence dating**: Carlson advocates for applying **optically stimulated luminescence (OSL)** to the Sphinx enclosure walls — the outer walls of the quarry trench, which were underground (protected from cosmic ray bombardment) until the blocks were removed, then exposed. OSL can date how long a stone surface has been exposed to the environment. This has not been done, to Carlson's knowledge.

**Key finding**: The Sphinx *ditch* walls (the quarry walls around the Sphinx body) — unlike the Sphinx body itself, which has undergone restoration campaigns — are essentially pristine. In places, Carlson observes **over 2 feet of limestone material has been eroded back**, which is incompatible with the desert conditions that have prevailed since Old Kingdom Egypt.

**Astronomical corroboration**: Jesse notes that Bauval/Hancock calculated ~10,500 BC as the date when the vernal equinox sunrise in the constellation Leo would have meant the Sphinx (a lion body) was looking directly at its namesake constellation. Carlson doesn't dispute this.

## Carlson's Revised Estimate — Discussion with Schoch (R7oyZGW99os)

In [[R7oyZGW99os]], Carlson provides a more explicit lower bound than in earlier interviews:

After ~2 months studying comparative limestone weathering (gravestones, surf-zone cliff tenon measurements, Smithsonian roof-cube studies, and the scientific literature on limestone recession rates), Carlson concluded the Sphinx enclosure walls require **a minimum of 20,000–30,000 years** of weathering — assuming rainfall averaging ~45 inches/year, comparable to recent wet periods in the Atlanta, GA area.

Carlson's discussion with [[robert-schoch]]: Carlson found that Schoch "hasn't looked in depth" at comparable limestone weathering studies from other field sites. Schoch publicly uses 7,000–9,000 years as a deliberately conservative academic position ("because he's in the academic community"), but "he's perfectly willing to believe it could be much older." Carlson treats Schoch's public number as a floor, not a best estimate.

**Implication**: A 20,000–30,000 year age for the Sphinx would push it into the Upper Paleolithic — during or before the peak of the last glacial maximum — at a time when, according to mainstream archaeology, humans were still pre-agricultural. The "cognitive revolution" and any sophisticated architecture from this period is anomalous under the standard model.

**Robert Schoch public position**: Uses 7,000–9,000 years (i.e., construction before 5000 BC, during the African Humid Period); states he is deliberately conservative.  
**Carlson's position based on his own weathering research**: Minimum 20,000–30,000 years.

**Schwaller de Lubicz lineage**: Carlson traces the water-erosion insight from [[schwaller-de-lubicz]] (first proposed) → [[john-anthony-west]] (popularized) → [[robert-schoch]] (geological analysis). He studied limestone weathering independently to test and validate the argument.
