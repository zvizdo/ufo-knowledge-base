# Piezoelectric Effect

The generation of electrical charge in certain crystalline materials (quartz, tourmaline, topaz, bone) when subjected to mechanical pressure or stress. The reverse piezoelectric effect describes the same materials deforming when an electrical field is applied. First formally described by Pierre and Jacques Curie in 1880.

In the KB, the piezoelectric effect appears primarily through Jeffrey Drum's terraforming hypothesis (documented in the [[51N8OxqZIWY]] transcript): rose quartz and granite — both strongly piezoelectric — are the dominant construction materials at major megalithic sites worldwide. Drum argues this is deliberate: the structures were designed to channel lightning strikes through piezoelectric stone into surrounding soil, electrolyzing minerals including natron (calcium and sodium carbonate) and dispersing them into agricultural land. The name "Egypt" derives from "Khemet" (the black land), named for extraordinarily fertile dark soil that Drum attributes partly to this electrochemical enrichment rather than solely to Nile alluvium.

The thesis connects the entire distributed megalithic network — dolmens and stone henges across Europe, North Africa, and the Americas — as terraforming infrastructure operating on geological timescales, with pyramid complexes as primary catalytic nodes. The connection to Baal worship at Baalbek (the site of the most anomalously massive megalithic construction) is supported by Baal's identity as a storm/lightning deity — the same phenomenon the site may have been engineered to harvest.
