# Helmholtz Resonance

Acoustic resonance phenomenon in cavity-and-neck geometries; in Drum's pyramid-as-chemical-factory thesis, the mechanism by which the King's Chamber's red Aswan granite resonance accelerated chemical reaction rates as a mechanical catalyst.

Helmholtz resonance occurs when a cavity with a constricted opening is excited at its natural frequency, producing pressure oscillations in the cavity. The King's Chamber, with its known acoustic resonance properties when struck, is hypothesized by [[jeffrey-drum]] to function as a Helmholtz resonator whose oscillating pressure field accelerates molecular collision rates in the chamber's reaction medium — the physical bridge between Christopher Dunn's [[acoustic-power-plant]] reading and the chemical-manufacturing reading.

## Physical Mechanism

A Helmholtz resonator consists of a rigid cavity connected to the outside via a narrow neck or port. When excited at the cavity's natural frequency (determined by cavity volume, neck length, and neck cross-section), the air mass in the neck oscillates against the spring-like compressibility of the air in the cavity. The classic acoustic example is the tone produced when blowing across a bottle's mouth.

For the King's Chamber interpretation, the cavity is the granite-lined chamber itself and the "neck" is the entrance passage and any internal shaft connections. The chamber's documented response to acoustic excitation in the ~110 Hz range (sometimes called the "King's Chamber resonance") is the empirical hook for the resonator interpretation. Whether the resonance was *intended* (Drum's thesis) or *incidental* to the construction (mainstream archaeology) is the interpretive question the Helmholtz framing tries to resolve.
