# DOE Human Genome Project

The US Department of Energy (DOE) was one of the two primary funding agencies for the Human Genome Project (HGP, 1990–2003), co-leading the project alongside the National Institutes of Health (NIH). The DOE contributed primarily through its national laboratory computing infrastructure — Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Los Alamos National Laboratory, and Oak Ridge National Laboratory provided the computational resources and sequencing capacity needed for the project. DOE's role demonstrates that the national labs, nominally nuclear weapons facilities, routinely operate as the computational and research backbone for major civilian science programs.

**DOE's biological mandate**: The DOE's involvement in human genomics derives from its longstanding mandate to study the biological effects of ionizing radiation — a legacy of the Manhattan Project and the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission studies on Hiroshima/Nagasaki survivors. This mandate gave DOE an interest in human DNA repair mechanisms, mutation rates, and genetic variation that predated the HGP and justified sustained biological research programs at weapons labs.

**Precedent for classified biology**: The HGP demonstrates that DOE national laboratories can run major genomics programs. This infrastructure precedent is part of the KB argument in [[doe-secret-science-nexus]] that classified analogs to the HGP — genomic analysis of recovered NHI biological materials, for example — would be logistically feasible and institutionally appropriate under the existing DOE national lab architecture. Unlike NIH (a civilian public health agency), DOE labs operate under DOE security structures compatible with the highest classification levels.

**Connection to Los Alamos**: [[los-alamos]] National Laboratory was a major HGP computing contributor. In the KB, Los Alamos is also connected to the [[scientists-targeting-pattern]] through several unexplained incidents involving LANL personnel. The co-presence of genomics infrastructure and UAP-adjacent incidents at LANL is a structural curiosity the KB documents without asserting causal connection.

**Epigenetics and genome legacy**: The HGP sequenced the genome but not the epigenome. Subsequent research has expanded into epigenetics — heritable gene expression changes not encoded in DNA sequence (→ [[epigenetic-inheritance]]). DOE labs have remained active in functional genomics research, extending the HGP legacy into the dimension of biological information beyond DNA sequence.
