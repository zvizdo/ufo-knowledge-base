# Claims-Thesis (Epistemological Status)

The "claims-thesis" designation is the KB's epistemological label for incidents or assertions that are: (a) not established by primary documentation (physical evidence, contemporaneous official records, or chain-of-custody documentation), (b) not demonstrated to be fabrications through positive evidence of falsification, and (c) resting primarily on secondhand or tertiary testimony from individuals who may have had genuine access, genuine experiences, or genuine beliefs but whose accounts cannot be independently verified.

**Distinction from fabrication**: A claims-thesis incident differs from a fabricated one in that the KB does not positively assert falsity — the claim *might* be true, and in some cases the witness background (military, intelligence, scientific) lends it conditional plausibility. But conditional plausibility is not verification.

**1954 Eisenhower meeting example**: The [[1954-eisenhower-ufo-meeting]] is the canonical KB claims-thesis case: Eisenhower's schedule has gaps consistent with an unannounced departure from Palm Springs, the dental appointment explanation was disputed at the time, and multiple independent sources claim to have been told about a meeting with non-human entities at Holloman AFB or Edwards AFB. Despite all this, the entire claim rests on secondhand and tertiary testimony without chain-of-custody documentation. The KB treats it as a contested claims-thesis rather than an established incident.

**Why the distinction matters**: The claims-thesis designation prevents the KB from treating popular or widely-cited claims as established facts, while also preventing reflexive dismissal of accounts with plausible witness backgrounds. It preserves the claim's evidentiary value (it was claimed, by whom, in what context) without affirming its truth.
