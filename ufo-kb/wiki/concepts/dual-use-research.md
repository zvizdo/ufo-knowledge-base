# Dual-Use Research

Dual-use research (formally "dual-use research of concern," DURC) describes scientific work that generates knowledge or technologies with simultaneous civilian/defensive applications and potential offensive or harmful applications — making it difficult to govern within normal open-science frameworks.

**Gain-of-function virology**: The EcoHealth Alliance/Wuhan Institute controversy ([[ecohealth-alliance]]) is the KB's primary dual-use governance failure case. Research enhancing pathogen transmissibility was conducted through a civilian NIH-funded nonprofit in a foreign facility, after DARPA specifically rejected the proposal on dual-use grounds. The governance framework failed to prevent the research while preventing transparency about it.

**Neuroweapons research**: [[neuroweapons]] development occupies the same dual-use space — research into neural stimulation, brain-computer interfaces, and directed energy health effects serves both therapeutic and targeting applications. The [[soviet-microwave-weapons]] program operated in this dual-use space; contemporary academic research on TMS, DEW health effects, and neural pattern recognition all have weaponization potential.

**Technology suppression nexus**: The [[suppressed-technology]] category often involves dual-use dynamics — technologies withheld from public markets because their weaponization potential is considered to outweigh their civilian benefit in the classification calculus. The scalar frequency healing device in the KB is explicitly attributed to FDA dual-use concerns and pharmaceutical opposition as the reason for its market exclusion.

**UAP reverse-engineering**: If UAP-derived technologies exist in classified programs, the reverse-engineering outputs would represent the most extreme dual-use case — technologies potentially orders of magnitude beyond conventional capability, with no civilian precedent for governance frameworks.

**Governance failures**: Dual-use research governance consistently fails at the boundary between open science and national security:
- Academic researchers discover militarily significant results and publish before security review
- Civilian funding flows to military-application research through nonprofit intermediaries
- Classification prevents safety oversight of highest-risk research
- Commercial interests suppress technologies to avoid competitive disruption
