# Skull Elongation

Skull elongation refers to two related phenomena: (1) the documented, widespread practice of artificial cranial deformation through infant head binding, found in cultures across Mesoamerica, South America, Egypt, and elsewhere; and (2) contested claims that certain skulls — particularly the Paracas skulls of Peru — have morphological characteristics inconsistent with artificial deformation alone, suggesting genetic or biological difference.

**Documented cranial deformation**: Artificial cranial deformation through infant head binding is well-documented archaeologically. It was practiced by pre-Columbian cultures in the Americas, certain ancient Egyptian noble classes, and other societies. The result is an elongated head shape but with normal skull volume for the age and size of the individual.

**Paracas skull controversy**: Skulls from the Paracas culture (Peru, ~800 BCE–100 CE) have been cited by alternative researchers as anomalous: allegedly larger cranial volume than artificial deformation alone can produce, different parietal plate structure, and (in claims by Brien Foerster and others) DNA analysis suggesting anomalous haplogroups. These claims have not been published in peer-reviewed journals with verified methodologies.

**Ancient astronaut context**: In [[ancient-astronaut-hypothesis]] frameworks, skull elongation is cited as evidence that ancient cultures were mimicking the phenotype of elongated-skull NHI (through binding) or that some ancient elites were of hybrid human-NHI origin. The elongated heads in Egyptian art (Akhenaten's family) and the Paracas mummies are primary exhibits.

**Epistemics**: The documented artificial deformation is science; the biological anomaly claims for specific skulls are not confirmed by independent genetic analysis with full chain of custody. Mainstream archaeology attributes the Paracas culture (800 BCE–100 CE) to a distinct pre-Inca coastal civilization in Peru.
