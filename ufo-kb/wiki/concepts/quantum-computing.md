# Quantum Computing

Quantum computing uses quantum-mechanical phenomena — superposition, entanglement, and quantum interference — to process information in ways that provide exponential speedups for specific computational problems (prime factorization, database search, quantum chemistry simulation) compared to classical computers.

**Physical basis**: Classical computers process information as bits (0 or 1). Quantum computers use qubits, which can be in a superposition of 0 and 1 simultaneously, allowing quantum algorithms to explore many solutions in parallel. Entanglement allows correlations between qubits that have no classical analog. Quantum interference allows constructive and destructive interference of probability amplitudes, amplifying correct answers and suppressing incorrect ones.

**Google Willow chip**: In late 2024, Google announced its Willow quantum chip, claiming it could perform a benchmark computation in under five minutes that would take classical supercomputers 10^25 years. [[mark-andreessen]] publicly stated that Willow had "proven" the [[many-worlds-interpretation]] — because a quantum computer's speedup can only be explained (in the Everett interpretation) by the computation being performed in parallel across multiple universes. Andreessen's statement attracted both interest and scientific pushback.

**NSC classified physics meeting**: Following Andreessen's Willow statement, he and other Silicon Valley technologists reportedly met with the [[national-security-council]] on "classified physics" — a meeting whose content has not been officially described but which has been cited in disclosure-adjacent discussions as potentially connected to the UAP physics question. The inference: if multiverse-leveraging computation is real, the physics implications extend beyond computing.

**UAP relevance**: Quantum computing appears in UAP discourse primarily through:
1. The Andreessen/Willow/multiverse chain, which implies real physics beyond classical description
2. Speculation that classified quantum computing programs have advanced further than public announcements, with implications for propulsion or communications
