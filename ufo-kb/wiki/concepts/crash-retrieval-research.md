# Crash Retrieval Research

Crash retrieval research is the investigative discipline focused on alleged recovery of non-human craft and associated materials (and biologics) by government or military actors. It is distinct from general UFO investigation in that it focuses specifically on physical recovery events and their aftermath — material handling, personnel debriefs, classification orders, and the chain of custody for recovered materials.

## Founding Figures

**[[leonard-stringfield]]** — Created the field with his "UFO Crash/Retrievals: Status Report" series beginning in 1978. Stringfield collected testimony from military and civilian witnesses to alleged recoveries, protecting source identities while publishing case summaries. He documented over 100 cases. His approach established the methodology: protecting sources, categorizing by corroboration level, and tracking patterns.

**[[stan-gordon]]** — Pennsylvania-based researcher who spent decades investigating the [[1965-kecksburg]] crash, pursuing NASA FOIA requests for Kecksburg records (NASA acknowledged records, then claimed they were "lost"), and interviewing local witnesses. Kecksburg is a core case in the retrieval literature.

## Government Acknowledgment

[[david-grusch]] testified to Congress in 2023 that the US operates multi-decade programs for retrieval and analysis of "non-human origin technical vehicles." This represents the first on-the-record government acknowledgment (under oath) consistent with crash retrieval research claims. See [[reverse-engineered-craft]].

## Corporate Custodians (Davis Account — LnAiNChnuEQ)

[[eric-davis]] provides the most operationally specific insider account of crash retrieval material custody in the KB:

- **[[trw]]** was the principal contractor for crash retrieval programs from ~early 1960s for approximately 29 years until 1989, when the CIA shut the programs down after no reverse-engineering progress.
- **[[lockheed-martin]]** retained crash retrieval materials after the 1989 shutdown (when they were supposed to be returned). [[glenn-gaffney]] (CIA S&T Director) was surprised this material still existed when it came up during AAWSAP.
- **[[bass]]** (Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies, the AAWSAP contractor) attempted to access the Lockheed materials under DIA auspices — Gaffney blocked the transfer.

This is the first KB account that names specific corporate custodians with insider attribution.

## Russia and China (Davis Account)

Davis also saw classified evidence (through an AAWSAP-era CIA asset) that **Russia has had at least one crash retrieval** — documents, photos, and reports assessed as legitimate. He places the specific event at "the end of the '80s." For China, Davis credits [[hal-puthoff]]'s DIA work as the source.

## AEA 1954 / "Born Secret" / DOE Jurisdiction (xnxasfyHtfo)

[[eric-davis]] explicitly confirms in [[xnxasfyHtfo]] that crash retrievals fall under the **Atomic Energy Act of 1954** born-secret provision. The moment recovered material with any radiological signature is found, it is classified at nuclear level automatically — no separate classification decision is needed. This routes crash retrieval research through **DOE jurisdiction** and then to aerospace contractors (Lockheed, Northrop) rather than through the Pentagon.

Davis frames this as the complete explanation for why crash retrieval programs end up with private contractors outside normal military oversight: it is not a loophole but the designed operation of the AEA. See [[atomic-energy-act-classification]] and [[doe-secret-science-nexus]].

## ~5 Contacts; No ARV Progress; Hidden in Plain Sight (xnxasfyHtfo)

Additional Davis claims from [[xnxasfyHtfo]]:
- He has **personally met approximately 5 people** from crash retrieval programs
- The programs had "several decades of best efforts" with no ARV production and no reverse-engineering progress — confirmed by classified briefing
- The program has **no physicists doing legitimate physics**, which Davis identifies as the structural explanation for failure
- The program is "hidden in plain sight" — it cannot be a sealed facility because people go in and out (plumbers, housekeeping staff); therefore it is hidden in the ordinary institutional environment
