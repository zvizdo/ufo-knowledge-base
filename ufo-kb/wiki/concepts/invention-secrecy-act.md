# Invention Secrecy Act

Federal law (35 U.S.C. §§ 181–188) enacted in 1951 that authorizes government agencies — primarily the Department of Defense and Department of Energy — to direct the Patent and Trademark Office to impose secrecy orders on patent applications. Under a secrecy order, the applicant cannot publish, export, or commercialize the invention; the order can be renewed indefinitely. Thousands of active secrecy orders exist at any given time; the USPTO reports the count annually, typically 4,000–6,000 active orders.

In the UAP context, the Invention Secrecy Act is cited as the legal mechanism through which patents on anomalous propulsion, novel materials, or energy systems adjacent to UAP reverse-engineering could be silently suppressed without alerting the applicant to the reason. An inventor who files a patent application for a device with unexpected performance characteristics — say, an electrogravitic propulsion system or a metamaterial with anomalous electromagnetic properties — may receive a secrecy order citing national security without ever learning whether a classified parallel program exists.

If confirmed, this would imply the government has a classified parallel program addressing the same technology — a pattern consistent with the [[anti-gravity-manhattan-project]] lineage in which private inventors' work was classified and replicated within the black-program world.
