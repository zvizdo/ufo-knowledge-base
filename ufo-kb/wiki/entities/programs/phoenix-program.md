# Phoenix Program

The Phoenix Program (Phụng Hoàng in Vietnamese) was a CIA-coordinated counterinsurgency program operating in South Vietnam (1965–1972), formally under the Civil Operations and Revolutionary Development Support (CORDS) program. Its purpose was to identify, capture, and neutralize the Viet Cong Infrastructure (VCI) — the political and organizational network that sustained the Viet Cong's armed operations.

**Operations**: Phoenix combined intelligence collection, interrogation (often under torture), targeted assassination, and defection incentive programs. Province Reconnaissance Units (PRUs) — South Vietnamese irregular forces often advised by CIA and US Special Forces — conducted most of the direct action. The program killed an estimated 20,000–40,000 suspected VCI members between 1965 and 1972, and captured or converted tens of thousands more.

**Michael Aquino connection**: [[michael-aquino]], US Army psychological operations officer and founder of the Temple of Set (a breakaway from Anton LaVey's Church of Satan), served in Vietnam and later in Army PSYOP commands. Aquino appears in the KB through his PSYOP work, his alleged involvement in the Presidio daycare abuse allegations (1987), and his theoretical writings on psychological operations. The Phoenix Program represents the institutional context for the CIA/military psychological operations tradition within which Aquino operated.

**Legacy and controversy**: Phoenix is one of the most controversial US government programs of the Cold War era. Critics argue it institutionalized torture and extrajudicial killing as policy; defenders argue it was militarily effective and legally authorized by wartime conditions. Its operational methods — population control databases, targeted human elimination based on informant intelligence — were studied by the US military and intelligence community as a model for future counterinsurgency programs.

**KB relevance**: Phoenix appears in the KB as context for the CIA's demonstrated capacity and willingness to conduct covert, lethal operations at scale — relevant to evaluating claims about the institutional culture that would manage UAP-related programs.
