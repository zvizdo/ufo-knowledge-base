# Tuskegee Experiment

US Public Health Service study — formally the "Tuskegee Study of Untreated Syphilis in the Negro Male" — conducted in Macon County, Alabama from 1932 to 1972. The study enrolled 399 Black men with latent syphilis and 201 uninfected controls. After penicillin became the standard of care for syphilis in 1947, researchers deliberately withheld treatment from the infected subjects in order to study disease progression through to autopsy. The study continued for 40 years. It was exposed by a whistleblower in 1972, triggering a public health scandal and leading to the National Research Act of 1974 and the Belmont Report — the foundational documents of modern research ethics in the United States.

**Scale and duration of deception**: The Tuskegee study deceived 399 people for 40 years about the nature of their condition and treatment, employed the US Public Health Service, the CDC, and the Tuskegee Institute, and killed an unknown number of subjects who died of untreatable syphilis after penicillin was available. The study was not the work of rogue actors — it was an official government program, supervised and funded for four decades.
