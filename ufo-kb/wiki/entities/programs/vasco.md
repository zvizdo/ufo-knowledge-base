# VASCO

**Vanishing and Appearing Sources during a Century of Observations.** Research project led by [[beatriz-villarroel]] at Stockholm University. The core methodology: compare historical astronomical survey plates (primarily from the [[palomar-observatory]], 1950–1956) against modern sky survey data to identify objects that appeared or vanished between the two epochs.

## Mandate

The original scientific motivation was to search for stellar technosignatures — stars that "go dark" without explanation (suggesting Dyson sphere construction or stellar engineering) or transient light sources with no natural explanation. By searching for anomalous change rather than steady-state sources, VASCO captures phenomena that conventional surveys miss by design.

## Key Finding

In the northern hemisphere Palomar plates (~780 hours of exposure time), the VASCO pipeline detected approximately **105,000 transient events** from 1950–1956. Extending to both hemispheres implies ~70,000 globally. The earth-shadow umbra test (see [[beatriz-villarroel]]) suggests 30–35% are real solar reflections rather than plate defects — implying tens of thousands of genuine anomalous events in pre-Sputnik skies.

## Rate

Approximately 1.1 transients per square degree per hour in the survey data — roughly 15,000 per year.

## Methodology Detail (lbGE3EC6StE)

Villarroel describes the comparison pipeline: **Mount Palomar plates from the early 1950s** vs. **modern PanSTARRS data** (from Hawaii), comparing the same sky positions 70 years apart. "Candidate transients" are anything present in the 1950s images but absent today — but many are noise, so statistical filters apply.

The "alignment test" (developed with statistician Lash): how many transients in a narrow band or line are needed for the arrangement itself to be statistically anomalous, beyond the individual transients' presence? This produced two statistically significant cases — July 27, 1952 (five objects on a narrow band) and July 19, 1952 (triple transient by Enrique Solano).

Dave Alman (media manager) connected the July 19 date to the [[1952-washington-dc-flyover]] — the first weekend of the DC flyover (July 19–20); July 27 aligns with the second weekend (July 26–27).
