# The Man Who Mastered Gravity

A biography of [[thomas-townsend-brown]] authored by [[paul-schatzkin]]. The book draws on primary sources unavailable to previous researchers — including extended interviews with Brown's daughter [[linda-brown]], a deathbed interview with [[jacque-corneau]] (the French officer who facilitated the 1956 vacuum experiments), and newly surfaced archival documents including the 1943 FBI file and the [[william-stevenson]] telegram.

## Significance

The book represents a decisive shift in the public record on Brown. Previous accounts were either hagiographic (uncritical admiration from hobbyists) or dismissive (mainstream physics characterizing him as a failed quack). Schatzkin's biography occupies neither position — it presents documented, cross-corroborated evidence that:

1. Brown's experiments produced genuine anomalous effects in vacuum, witnessed by credible institutional figures.
2. His work was deliberately suppressed and absorbed into classified black aerospace programs.
3. The "amateur quack" narrative was at least partially engineered — including by Brown himself (the "wounded prairie chicken" disinformation routine).

Jesse Michaels describes the book as "a vindication of Brown, a resounding defense of his work, one that points to a new paradigm of science proven experimentally and witnessed by the highest levels of American government — just to get sequestered, confiscated, and go into deep black aerospace."

## Key Primary Sources Used

- [[linda-brown]] — extensive interviews; personal recollections of Teller demo, Sarbacher relationship, Nassau meetings
- [[jacque-corneau]] — deathbed interview confirming 1956 Paris vacuum results
- 1943 FBI file — identifies Brown as "the man who knew more about radar detection than any individual in the US Navy"
- Stevenson telegram — proof of Brown's Germany mission and MI6 connection
- Navy FOIA responses — documents the institutional stonewalling and deliberate obscuring of Brown's service record
