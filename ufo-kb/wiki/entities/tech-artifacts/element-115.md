# Element 115 (Moscovium)

The super-heavy element [[bob-lazar]] describes as the key fuel/reactor material in the [[s4-sport-model]]'s propulsion system. Lazar publicly identified it in 1989 as a stable, naturally occurring heavy element with an atomic number of 115 — sixteen years before the first laboratory synthesis of moscovium in 2003.

## Lazar's Account of Element 115 at S4

At S4, Lazar and [[barry-castillo]] characterised the element using:
- **Atomic absorption spectroscopy**
- **X-ray diffraction and X-ray imaging**

The X-ray imaging revealed a hollow tube structure running through the reactor with a loop at the base — theorized to be a small cyclotron or linear accelerator embedded within the reactor.

Lazar knew the specific stable isotope at the time of his work at S4 but has since forgotten it. This is an open question with potentially significant scientific implications — if recoverable, a stable moscovium isotope would be both Nobel-level and potentially reproducible.

## How It Works (Lazar's Model)

1. The element 115 mass is placed in the reactor.
2. The reactor bombards the element 115 with protons (via the internal cyclotron structure).
3. Proton bombardment creates **element 116** (livermorium).
4. Element 116 immediately decays.
5. The decay produces a **gravity/force wave** — the propulsive output.
6. This wave is amplified and directed by the three emitters beneath the craft.

Lazar's use of the phrase "gravity wave" is his interpretation of what he was told and observed; he increasingly believes the force may be a previously unidentified fifth or sixth fundamental force, not gravity per se.

## The Isotope Problem

The moscovium isotopes synthesized in terrestrial laboratories (2003, GSI Helmholtz Centre, Darmstadt; later confirmed by JINR Dubna) are all highly unstable, with half-lives measured in milliseconds. Lazar's account requires a **stable, naturally occurring isotope** — something that does not exist in published nuclear physics.

Critics cite this discrepancy. Advocates note that the synthesized isotopes are produced via heavy-ion bombardment of heavy actinides (curium or americium targets) — not the natural nucleosynthesis pathways that might produce a "magic number" stable superheavy isotope. The theoretical possibility of a stable island of superheavy nuclei in this region of the periodic table is recognized but unproven.

## Chemical Properties and Bismuth Connection

Element 115 sits in **Group 15** of the periodic table, sharing five valence electrons with:
- Nitrogen
- Phosphorus
- Arsenic
- Antimony
- Bismuth

Bismuth (element 83) is the stable Group 15 element immediately below antimony. It is notable for:
- Highest diamagnetism of any stable element
- Strong spin-orbit coupling
- Properties as a natural topological insulator
- Bismuth titanate: high-performance electret
- Bismuth ferrite: simultaneously ferroelectric and magnetic

The chemical family relationship between element 115 and bismuth — combined with the [[1957-ubatuba-orb]] crash site magnesium-bismuth samples being analyzed by [[garry-nolan]] at Stanford — makes bismuth a significant thread in the S4 propulsion picture.

## Vindication vs. Coincidence Debate

- **Advocates**: Lazar naming element 115 in 1989 and the subsequent 2003 synthesis is a predictive hit. The element's placement in Group 15 means the chemist who synthesized it (confirming its five valence electrons and bismuth-like properties) was, in a narrow sense, confirming what Lazar said.
- **Critics**: The synthesized isotopes are unstable. The periodic table ordering was already known theoretically in 1989 — predicting element 115 would go in Group 15 was not difficult. The naming is coincidence, not confirmation.

The debate remains unresolved. The question of a stable isotope is the load-bearing scientific claim that cannot yet be tested.
