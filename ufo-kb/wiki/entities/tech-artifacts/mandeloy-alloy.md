# Mandeloy Alloy

A family of nickel-based superalloys developed in the 1990s to solve one of the most persistent problems in advanced rocket propulsion: creating a metallic alloy that could survive the extreme pressure and oxidizing environment of **oxygen-rich staged-combustion rocket engines** without burning or fracturing.

## Technical Background

High-performance rocket engines require high combustion pressure and an oxygen-rich pre-burner to drive turbopumps. This creates a brutal metallurgical problem:
- Alloys strong enough to maintain structural integrity in that environment tended to combust in the oxygen-rich atmosphere
- Alloys that didn't combust lacked the structural properties needed to contain the engine

For decades, the US could not solve this. The result: the US relied on Russia's **RD-180 engine** for the most sensitive national security space launches — a strategic vulnerability.

**The breakthrough:** [[monica-resza]] and [[dallas-hardwick]], working at the Rockwell Science Center in the 1990s, engineered a nickel-based alloy — **Mandeloy** (name formed from the first three letters of each co-inventor's name) — that achieved the required combination of structural integrity and combustion resistance.

## Development and Scale-Up

- **1990s** — Mandeloy invented at Rockwell Science Center
- **1999** — [[afrl]] begins co-funding development
- **Mandeloy 100 and 200** — scaled to a family of alloys for different temperature/pressure conditions; partnership between AFRL and the team at Pratt & Whitney Rocketdyne
- [[dallas-hardwick]] embedded in AFRL's Materials Directorate at [[wright-patterson]] through 2012 — the period when [[neil-mccasland]] arrived to command AFRL
- Mandeloy entered production in **Aerojet Rocketdyne's AR1 engine** — the US-built replacement for the Russian RD-180; kerosene-fueled; critical for national security space launches

## UAP / Exotic Metallurgy Context

Some researchers place Mandeloy in a lineage of exotic nickel alloys they trace to the Roswell crash of 1947:
- 1947: Jesse Marcel Jr. describes Roswell debris as thin, foil-like metal that held a lighter without burning
- 1949: Wright-Patterson's Battelle Memorial Institute receives a contract to study nickel-titanium alloys — two years after Roswell
- 1959: **[[nitinol]]** (shape-memory alloy, ~45% titanium) developed at the Naval Ordnance Laboratory
- 1990s: Mandeloy (nickel-based, only ~1–4% titanium; very different metallurgical neighborhood)

The source acknowledges this lineage while noting Mandeloy's patents cite conventional antecedents (Hastelloy X-214, Monel alloy K500) — not overtly exotic. The hypothesis is that recovered NHI materials inspired the research direction without being directly cloned. This is speculative and not established by any primary documentation.

What is established:
- Mandeloy was developed under AFRL contracts
- The same AFRL that is the most consistently cited institutional site for UAP material storage and reverse engineering
- Both its co-inventor (Resza) and the AFRL commander who oversaw its development (McCasland) have now disappeared
