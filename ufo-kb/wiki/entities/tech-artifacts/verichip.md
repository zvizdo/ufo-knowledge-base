# VeriChip

VeriChip was a subsidiary of Applied Digital Solutions that manufactured and marketed subcutaneous RFID microchips for human implantation. The FDA approved the device for human use in October 2004, making it the first human-implantable RFID chip to receive FDA clearance in the United States. The company rebranded as PositiveID Corporation in 2010 and largely discontinued the human implant product line around the same period.

## Description

The VeriChip is a passive RFID transponder encased in a medical-grade glass capsule approximately 11mm long and 2.1mm in diameter. It contains no battery and is activated only when an external RFID reader provides power via inductive coupling. The chip stores a 16-digit identification number that can be read by compatible scanners.

**Implantation method**: VeriChip used a proprietary dual-needle injection system. The device was pre-loaded in an applicator containing two needles — one to create the insertion track and one to deposit the chip. This dual-needle configuration produces two distinct puncture marks at the injection site, with a predictable separation distance and diameter.

Intended applications included patient identification in medical settings (linking patients to electronic health records), personal security access systems, and tracking for individuals with Alzheimer's disease or other conditions where identification might be difficult.

## Corporate Chain

The corporate structure McKinnon researched:

- **Applied Digital Solutions** → parent company of VeriChip
- **Digital Angel Corporation** → another Applied Digital subsidiary; the chip manufacturing arm
- A **Raytheon subsidiary** supplied the chip component used in VeriChip's devices

The Raytheon connection — a major US defense contractor specializing in missile systems, radar, and intelligence electronics — is the element [[gary-mckinnon]] emphasizes as evidence that VeriChip was not merely a commercial medical device company but had defense-intelligence provenance.

## Relevance to McKinnon — The Heel Wound

In 2006, several years after McKinnon's arrest and during his extradition fight, McKinnon was awakened from deep sleep by sharp pain in his left heel. Upon examination in the morning he found:

- **Two perfectly circular holes**, approximately 5mm in diameter each
- A **triangular skin flap** between the punctures
- No other trauma or explanation

Months later — independently — McKinnon encountered information about VeriChip's dual-needle injection procedure and recognized that the physical pattern (two circular holes, specific spacing, skin flap from the introducer) was an exact match for a VeriChip implantation event.

Subsequently, two small bumps appeared at the heel site and have since migrated, which McKinnon interprets as consistent with an implanted device moving through soft tissue (a recognized phenomenon with subcutaneous foreign bodies).

McKinnon's interpretation is that he was implanted with a tracking device by a government or intelligence agency — not by extraterrestrials — using commercially available VeriChip technology. He explicitly distinguishes this from alien abduction scenarios.

## Roger Leir Connection

[[roger-leir]], the podiatric surgeon who performed over 17 surgical removals of alleged implants from UFO experiencers, is cited by McKinnon as the appropriate specialist to examine his heel. Leir's surgical and analytical protocol was designed precisely for cases of this type — identifying, removing, and laboratory-analyzing suspected subcutaneous foreign bodies under sterile conditions. McKinnon notes with regret that Leir died in 2014, before McKinnon connected with him.

The procedural irony: if McKinnon's wound reflects government surveillance (VeriChip) rather than NHI contact, it is still within the scope of Leir's work — the question of provenance (government vs. NHI) is separate from the question of whether a physical implant is present.

## Analysis/Findings

No independent medical investigation of McKinnon's heel has been publicly documented. The analytical questions that would resolve the case:

1. **RF scanning**: Do the heel bumps emit any RF signal? A passive RFID device would respond to a compatible reader at the chip's frequency
2. **Imaging**: X-ray or MRI of the area might reveal a metal-containing foreign body
3. **Surgical retrieval**: If an object were removed and analyzed, its composition would distinguish VeriChip (glass, specific metal alloy), other commercial RFID, or something else

None of these steps has been taken publicly. McKinnon himself does not appear to have pursued medical investigation — possibly due to the legal constraints of his situation, or a concern about what such investigation would reveal or invite.
