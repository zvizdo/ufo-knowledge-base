# James L. Tuck

> *Stub. Expand with academic publications, fusion-research history, Manhattan Project role, and any additional UFO/UAP-related primary documentation as it enters the KB.*

British-American physicist (1910–1980); Manhattan Project veteran; principal architect of the Perhapsatron stellarator under Project Sherwood at Los Alamos in the 1950s; senior staff in [[los-alamos-national-laboratory|Los Alamos]]'s P (Physics) Division through his 1973 retirement. Open-literature reputation as a serious ball-lightning experimentalist.

## UAP/UFO-Related Documentation

The DOE 2026 release of [[doe-uap-d2-james-tuck-correspondence-1970-1976]] (archive index A-83-001-2-1, accessioned 1983) confirms Tuck as an active UFO-curious physicist:

- **December 1970** — Tuck wrote to the U.S. Army Engineering School at Fort Belvoir VA (Department of Mechanical and Technical Equipment) requesting "the recipe that was used for the simulated atomic bomb demonstrations" because "we are interested in the large atmospheric vortices which are produced as reported in the book *Scientific Study of Unidentified Flying Objects* by Dr. Edward U. Condon" — i.e., to attempt experimental reproduction of the vortex phenomenology catalogued in the [[condon-report]].
- **November 1970** — Tuck received a handwritten letter from a Los Alamos colleague reporting "several sightings of green lights" at Los Alamos during 1948–1951 in the [[jemez-mountains|Jemez Mountains]], and a daytime five-object formation sighting over Los Alamos — i.e., Tuck was actively soliciting Los Alamos site-internal recollections of the green-fireballs corpus.
- **November 28, c.1976** — Tuck received a letter referencing his "interesting report on ball lightning" and enclosing comments from James M. McCampbell's *UFOLOGY* (1976) chapter "Flight and Propulsion," explicitly tying Einstein's late-career unified-field-theory pursuit to UFO propulsion physics.

These documents place Tuck inside the small set of senior physics-establishment figures actively cross-referencing nuclear-effects research, ball lightning, and the UAP corpus in the early 1970s.
