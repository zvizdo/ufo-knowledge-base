# Michael Shermer

Michael Shermer is an American author, science historian, and professional skeptic. He founded the Skeptics Society in 1992 and has served as editor-in-chief of Skeptic magazine since its launch the same year. He was also a monthly columnist for Scientific American for many years ("Skeptic" column, 1997–2018), giving him one of the highest-profile mainstream platforms for skeptical analysis. His books include Why People Believe Weird Things (1997) and The Believing Brain (2011), which offer cognitive and evolutionary explanations for supernatural and conspiratorial thinking.

On UAP specifically, Shermer consistently argues that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, that human perceptual and memory systems are unreliable especially under novel or ambiguous conditions, and that the cultural template of alien visitors predisposes witnesses to interpret unusual stimuli through that lens. He treats the current UAP disclosure movement with measured skepticism: acknowledging that some phenomena remain genuinely unexplained while opposing the inferential leap to extraterrestrial origin without physical proof. Within the KB's [[credibility-frameworks]] analysis, Shermer represents the rigorous skeptical anchor — his arguments are the standard that UAP proponents must engage rather than dismiss.

## Hancock Debate and Subsequent Update

Shermer debated [[graham-hancock]] on the Joe Rogan Experience in **2017**. After the debate, Shermer's tone shifted. When Hancock's Netflix docu-series *[[ancient-apocalypse]]* aired in 2022, Shermer **emailed Hancock** to say he felt Hancock had made a good case for "my basic proposition that we're missing an episode of the human story." Hancock cites this in [[dfPfPB601hw]] as evidence of Shermer's intellectual integrity: his willingness to step out of an entrenched position when evidence warrants.

This update does not constitute full conversion — Shermer's overall position remains skeptical — but it represents a meaningful shift from the pre-2017 "pseudoarchaeology" framing.

## Shermer on Personal Anomalous Experience

Hancock mentions a well-known Shermer anecdote: on his wedding night (or close to it), a mechanical clock that had been an heirloom from his late father-in-law — broken for years — spontaneously began playing music. Shermer, a committed materialist, wrote about this as an "anomalous mystical moment" requiring he "make room for these sorts of experiences" even within a scientific worldview.
