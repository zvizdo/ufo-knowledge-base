# Greg Rogers

Dr. Gregory Rogers is a recently retired chief flight surgeon for the US Air Force and NASA, and former director of aerospace medicine at the [[45th-space-wing]], based at [[patrick-air-force-base]] and [[cape-canaveral]]. He served as senior flight surgeon for the astronaut rescue and recovery team at [[kennedy-space-center]], flying aboard Jolly 1 for every Space Shuttle launch and landing. He came forward publicly in May 2024 (immediately following his April 30 retirement) with a firsthand 1992 account of watching a reverse-engineered saucer craft under test via a closed-circuit TV feed.

## Background

Rogers' career gave him unusual breadth of access across Cape Canaveral's contractor ecosystem. As chief of aerospace medicine for the 45th Space Wing, he managed the flight medicine program across Cape Canaveral Air Force Station, Patrick AFB, and the Eastern Space and Missile Center. His role under the [[egng]] prime contractor arrangement required him to visit roughly 80% of the buildings on Cape Canaveral — giving him an unusually wide operational footprint compared to personnel who were compartmentalized to single sites.

Additional career highlights:
- Flew helicopters and F-16s; served with 9th Strategic Reconnaissance Wing aircraft
- Flew search and rescue missions over the South Atlantic
- Served in Germany with Blackhawk operations
- Supported AFTAC operations across the South Atlantic and South America
- Participated in MODATA exercises (ocean astronaut bail-out rescue drills)
- Advocated successfully (over ~2 years) to switch astronaut survival equipment from red to green chem lights — a direct safety improvement for nighttime ocean rescue
- Diagnosed with level-one autism and PTSD (wartime service); writes openly about both in his book

## The 1992 Sighting

Rogers describes the incident in [[TNtlzEnl8rA]]. See the full incident record at [[1992-cape-canaveral-saucer]].

**Core sequence:**
On an EG&G-escorted tour of Cape Canaveral buildings, Rogers' escort left slightly ahead of him. In the brief gap (60–90 seconds alone), a USAF major — who apparently recognized Rogers from a prior flight physical — invited him into a computer console room, locked the door, closed all louvered blinds, and pulled up a closed-circuit TV feed on screen.

The feed showed a **pearly-white saucer approximately 20 feet across**, shaped like a modified egg (symmetric, not offset), with a completely smooth surface — no rivets, no seams, no windows. Black painted rectangles at the beam line and 3/6/9/12 o'clock positions appeared to be reference markers for motion tracking. The craft bore "US Air Force" markings and an Air Force flight insignia (revealed during rotation).

The craft then:
- Lifted off the floor with no discernible mechanism — "light as a feather"
- Hovered 3 ft up
- Rotated clockwise 360°, then counterclockwise 360°, returning to start
- Moved left and right, forward and backward — all motions smooth and precise
- Generated continuous audible and visible electrostatic discharges across its surface

Three umbilical lines attached to a pole at the craft's apex. Rogers interprets these as external power/control inputs, indicating the craft was a prototype not yet self-sufficient.

When Rogers asked why anyone would build a craft in that configuration, the major's response: **"We got it from them."**

The session lasted ~15 minutes total before a lieutenant colonel and two captains knocked and entered. The major claimed the doctor visit was about a skin lesion. Rogers chose not to report the incident, reasoning that no classification marking appeared on the feed and that reporting would create a career-threatening chain of events with uncertain outcome.

## Decision Not to Report

Rogers explicitly weighs his decision in the interview:
- The feed bore no classification marking; he could not definitively establish an unauthorized disclosure had occurred
- Whoever showed the major the feed likely already knew about it, meaning reporting might implicate the chain of command
- In 1992 culture, "you just didn't report flying saucers"
- The uncertainty about where the feed was coming from meant he couldn't even guarantee what exactly he'd be reporting
- Career risk was severe and certain; outcome of reporting was uncertain

> "I drove 30 minutes down to Patrick thinking this whole time... I am not going to report this thing."

## Motivations for Coming Forward

Rogers was triggered to go public by [[david-grusch]]'s 2023 congressional testimony. Grusch stated that witnesses to reverse-engineering programs needed to come forward to corroborate his claims. When government officials responded that "we have never had a craft like that in our inventory," Rogers decided he had a direct answer to that claim.

> "I've got his six o'clock covered."

Rogers retired April 30, 2024. His first public disclosure — via UK Daily Mail journalist [[josh-boswell]] — appeared May 4, 2024. He subsequently appeared on multiple platforms including the Letto Files (with [[chris-leto]]) and American Alchemy.

Rogers explicitly acknowledges that while he cannot confirm all of Grusch's claims, he can confirm the existence of a craft under test — and that government denials contradicting that are false.

## PTSD and Openness

Rogers has level-one autism and PTSD from wartime medical service. He discusses both openly, including in his book. He frames this disclosure preemptively — aware that PTSD was weaponized against Grusch — and advocates broadly for military personnel to seek help rather than conceal mental health struggles.

One defining incident: during deployment, Rogers collected the remains of a pilot friend who died in a crash, was counseling other personnel all afternoon, and only hours later discovered part of the pilot's brain on his boot. He disposed of it in a pot-belly stove and lost ~4 hours to a dissociative episode. He calls this "one of the stories that's not a good story."

## Craft Comparison to Bob Lazar's Sport Model

Rogers volunteers the comparison: if you take [[bob-lazar]]'s "Sport Model" description and smooth out the corners and cockpit protrusion, you get something very close to what Rogers saw — smooth, pearly-white, no visible seams or windows, disc-shaped but slightly modified-egg in profile.

Rogers is careful: "I don't know if they're at all related, but boy, it would be a lot like it."

## Book: *We Got It From Them*

Rogers has written a book titled *We Got It From Them* — borrowing the major's exact phrase — covering his UFO testimony alongside military career stories (including PTSD). The UFO content alternates with military vignettes to give civilian readers a feel for military life. One full section is devoted to PTSD advocacy.
