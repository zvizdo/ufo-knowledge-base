# Chase Brandon

## Background

Chase Brandon served 35 years at the CIA as a clandestine case officer and, later, as the CIA's official liaison to the entertainment industry — a role that placed him at the intersection of classified knowledge and Hollywood production. On leaving the CIA, he made unusually explicit public claims about the reality of the Roswell crash.

## Notable Claims

### Roswell Archive Claim

In 2012, Brandon publicly stated he had personally reviewed documents in the CIA's Historical Intelligence Collection that confirmed the [[1947-roswell]] crash involved a real craft and real bodies — "not weather balloons." He offered no declassified documentation but spoke on the record.

> ⚠ **Conflict**: The CIA's official response to Brandon's 2012 claim was that they could find no documents matching his description in the Historical Intelligence Collection. No other official has corroborated access to such documents. Brandon provided no photographic or documentary evidence. The claim is therefore currently uncorroborated firsthand testimony from a single CIA-affiliated source with strong incentive to promote his novel.

### Allegorical Disclosure Signal

In the same year, Brandon published an allegorical novel with a Francis Bacon epigraph: *"Truth is so hard to tell, it sometimes needs fiction to make it plausible."* This is highlighted in [[09KP8XVf5nY]] as a pattern consistent with other intelligence-adjacent figures using fiction or coded public statements to signal classified knowledge they cannot directly disclose. The host reads it as an institutional disclosure strategy: signal through the fiction that you're signaling.
