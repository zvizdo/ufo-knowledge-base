# Halton Arp

American astrophysicist (1927–2013). Worked at Carnegie Observatories (Palomar and Mount Wilson) before being denied further telescope time due to his heterodox findings, subsequently working at the Max-Planck-Institute für Astrophysik in Germany.

## Notable Claims

Arp observed pairs of galaxies that appeared to be physically interacting — exchanging gas — but had wildly different redshifts. Under standard cosmology, redshift encodes distance: two galaxies with different redshifts should be at different distances and cannot be interacting. Arp published an **Atlas of Peculiar Galaxies** (1966) documenting these anomalous pairs.

The mainstream response was severe: his telescope access at Carnegie was revoked. He relocated to Germany to continue his work. Villarroel describes his situation as the 1980s analog of the contemporary controversy around [[avi-loeb]] and Oumuamua — a credentialed astronomer attacked by peers for observations that challenge foundational cosmological assumptions.

Arp's concept of **non-cosmological redshifts** (also called anomalous redshifts) — the idea that redshift may not purely encode recessional velocity — remains outside mainstream cosmology but has never been rigorously refuted by studying the actual object samples he identified.

## Influence on Villarroel

[[beatriz-villarroel]] credits Arp as the figure who first made her ask: *"Has anyone ever searched for galaxies or stars that vanish?"* Her essay based on Arp's ideas, written as an undergraduate, ended with a quasar "vanishing" — which became the seed question for [[vasco]]. The Arp controversy demonstrated to her that mainstream attack on heterodox findings does not constitute disproof.
