# Walther Gerlach

German experimental physicist (1889–1979). With Otto Stern, designed and conducted the **1922 Stern-Gerlach experiment** at the University of Frankfurt — demonstrating the quantization of electron spin angular momentum and establishing that quantum mechanical spin has only discrete values (+1/2 or -1/2), not a continuous range. The experiment is one of the foundational demonstrations of quantum mechanics.

**WWII role**: During WWII, Gerlach served as plenipotentiary (Bevollmächtigter) for nuclear physics in the Reichsforschungsrat (Reich Research Council) and as a senior scientific administrator in the [[uranverein]] — the German nuclear weapons program. His role was primarily administrative: coordinating nuclear research across German universities and laboratories, managing the distribution of heavy water and uranium, and representing nuclear physics to the Nazi regime. Whether he had scientific knowledge of UAP-adjacent physics work remains unknown.

**Die Glocke connection**: In UFO/secret-tech discourse, Gerlach is one of the scientists most frequently named as the alleged scientific director or consultant for [[die-glocke]] — the hypothesized Nazi anti-gravity / rotating mercury-vortex device. The claim originates primarily in Igor Witkowski's book *The Truth About the Wunderwaffe* (2000), based on his interpretation of postwar Polish intelligence material. Gerlach's name appears in Witkowski's reconstruction alongside Hans Kammler ([[kammler-stab]]) as the SS organizational patron.

**Evidence status**: No contemporaneous document has been produced naming Gerlach as involved with Die Glocke. The claim is Witkowski-source-tier — a secondary account derived from Polish intelligence material whose provenance and completeness are unverified. [[eric-wargo]] treats the Gerlach/Glocke material with appropriate caution. The KB notes the claim without endorsing it.

**Post-war career**: After the war, Gerlach was detained in Operation Alsos and Farm Hall alongside other German nuclear scientists. He eventually returned to Germany and continued academic work at Munich, receiving honors for his quantum mechanics contributions.
