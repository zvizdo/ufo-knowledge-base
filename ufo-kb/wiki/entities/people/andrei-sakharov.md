# Andrei Sakharov

Soviet theoretical physicist (1921–1989). The primary architect of the Soviet hydrogen bomb (the "Sakharov device") and designer of the **Tokamak** configuration for controlled thermonuclear fusion — the leading candidate today for commercial fusion energy. Later became a prominent dissident and Nobel Peace Prize laureate (1975) for his human rights advocacy.

## Physics and GEM Convergence

[[john-brandenberg]] specifically cites Sakharov in the context of gravity-electromagnetism unification:

- Sakharov independently derived that **gravity can be understood as an electromagnetic vacuum effect** — a form of radiation pressure from zero-point fluctuations. This is conceptually identical to the Sakharov-Puthoff approach that [[hal-puthoff]] also developed.
- Brandenburg notes that Sakharov and Teller "thought down the same passages… looked at the data and came to the same conclusions even though they were completely isolated from each other" — applied to both the hydrogen bomb path and, implicitly, to vacuum physics.
- Sakharov also commented on zero-point energy: he suggested the only way to "tap" zero-point energy would be to maintain a black hole and feed it matter — releasing some energy as Hawking radiation; anything less exotic would not work. Brandenburg cites this as a reason to be skeptical of free-energy claims.

## Tokamak

The Tokamak (a Russian acronym for "toroidal chamber with magnetic coils") is the dominant approach to controlled fusion today. All major contemporary fusion projects (ITER, etc.) use this configuration. Brandenburg describes Sakharov as the designer and notes that Teller, in a parallel to their H-bomb work, also believed in controlled fusion.

## Teller-Sakharov Parallel

Brandenburg draws an explicit parallel:
- Both Teller (American, Hungarian-born) and Sakharov worked on hydrogen bombs for their respective superpowers, each believing the other side would not stop.
- Sakharov's examination of Teller's declassified communications later confirmed: "Teller was right. We would have never stopped working on the hydrogen bomb."
- The two men eventually met; both recognized they had reached the same conclusions independently across complete information isolation.
