# Lincoln LaPaz

Meteorite expert and astronomer at the University of New Mexico. Before leading the Near-Earth Satellite Project, headed [[project-twinkle]] — an Air Force investigation into unexplained green fireballs appearing over US nuclear sites, including Los Alamos and Holloman Air Force Base. His conclusion from Project Twinkle was that these fireballs were **not a known natural phenomenon** and that they appeared to propel themselves intelligently. He advocated for serious government investigation and briefed USAF and FBI.

LaPaz's scientific specialty (meteorite analysis) made him the natural choice for investigating unusual luminous phenomena. His Project Twinkle conclusion — that the green fireballs were artificial — was remarkable given his credentialed mainstream standing.

## Near-Earth Satellite Project

Co-led with [[clyde-tombaugh]] at [[white-sands-missile-range]] in 1953–1954. Ostensibly tasked with identifying small natural satellites (mini-moons or captured asteroids). In August 1954, LaPaz published a statement in *Aviation Week and Space Technology* confirming that two unknown objects had been identified — but simultaneously claiming they were natural asteroids. Problems with this claim:
- The objects never appear again in any Near-Earth object or asteroid literature
- The discovery of two natural Earth satellites would have been a major astrophysical finding requiring follow-up
- The project's own final report concludes there are **no natural satellites of Earth** — directly contradicting the "natural asteroid" explanation
- This directly corroborates [[donald-keyhoe]]'s 1953 claim that the Air Force was tracking two unknown artificial satellites

## Significance

LaPaz stands at the intersection of two anomalous phenomena: green fireballs at nuclear sites (Project Twinkle) and unknown pre-Sputnik orbital objects (Near-Earth Satellite Project). In both cases, his findings suggest genuine anomalies that were subsequently explained away with unconvincing cover stories.
