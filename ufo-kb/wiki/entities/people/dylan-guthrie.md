# Dylan Guthrie

Attorney who published a lengthy article in the **Harvard Law Review** that includes a section addressing the prosecution risk for individuals who disclose classified information to Congress. [[kirk-mcconnell]] cites Guthrie's research in the context of arguing that the fear of prosecution is largely unfounded.

## Key Findings

Guthrie researched the historical record and found **zero instances** in which a person was indicted or prosecuted for having revealed classified information to Congress through appropriate, secure channels. (This is distinct from going public — the claim is specific to classified briefings delivered to the appropriate congressional oversight committees.)

McConnell interprets this as legally significant: the constitutional tension between executive branch classification authority and Congress's legislatively mandated right to be fully informed of national security matters creates a bind that makes prosecution politically and legally implausible. To prosecute, the government would have to:
1. Admit it has been concealing the relevant information from Congress for decades
2. Violate the implicit separation-of-powers compact that protects congressional oversight
3. Invite congressional outrage and a full-scale investigation

## Relevance to UAP Whistleblowers

This research directly addresses the [[whistleblower-protection-gap]] catchphrase in UAP disclosure circles: potential witnesses report fearing prosecution above all other deterrents. Guthrie's analysis suggests this fear is historically unwarranted for disclosures made to Congress, as opposed to going fully public.

McConnell notes this doesn't eliminate the fear (it's easy to say when you're not the one at risk), but thinks it should significantly reduce the legal deterrence for potential UAP witnesses.
