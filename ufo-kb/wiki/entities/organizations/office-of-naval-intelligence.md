# Office of Naval Intelligence (ONI)

The Office of Naval Intelligence is the oldest continuously operating intelligence organization in the United States, established in 1882. It operates under the Department of the Navy and is responsible for maritime intelligence — collecting, analyzing, and producing intelligence on foreign naval forces, shipping, and ocean environments. ONI is headquartered at the National Maritime Intelligence Center in Suitland, Maryland.

## Overview

ONI's mandate encompasses foreign naval order of battle, undersea warfare, maritime patrol and reconnaissance, foreign technology acquisition in the naval domain, and counterintelligence related to naval programs. As the Navy's primary intelligence arm, it handles classified information about naval capabilities, personnel, operations, and research programs.

Within the broader US intelligence community, ONI is a component of the Defense Intelligence Enterprise and shares finished intelligence products with other agencies. It maintains liaison relationships with allied naval intelligence services.

## Programs/Activities

### Role in the McKinnon Case

ONI's involvement in the [[gary-mckinnon]] case is one of the most documented instances of its alleged role in UAP-related information control:

1. **Hard drive seizure at arrest**: When McKinnon was arrested in March 2002 by the UK National High-Tech Crime Unit (NHTCU), his computer equipment — including hard drives containing data from his sessions — was physically seized not by the FBI or a civilian law enforcement agency, but by ONI. This is anomalous. Computer crime investigations in the UK involving US civilian charges would typically involve FBI liaison, not the Office of Naval Intelligence. The ONI seizure indicates the agency had specific, pre-arranged institutional interest in the materials.

2. **The NHTCU-ONI Washington visit**: After McKinnon's arrest, UK NHTCU officers traveled to ONI's Washington offices. McKinnon describes this visit as the pivotal moment when the legal situation transformed: the initial UK assessment (6 months community service) was replaced by a full US extradition push under 7 counts with potential sentences of 70 years. Whatever was communicated between the NHTCU and ONI in Washington caused the US government to treat this as a matter of the highest institutional priority.

3. **The spreadsheet was on a Navy network**: The [[non-terrestrial-officers]] spreadsheet — McKinnon's most significant claimed find — was located on a US Navy network, not Army, Air Force, or a civilian agency network. This gives ONI a specific institutional jurisdictional claim over that data.

4. **Evidence never returned**: McKinnon's hard drives, containing his downloaded copy of the spreadsheet, have never been returned or publicly examined. McKinnon states that attempting to retrieve them through legal channels has been fruitless.

### UAP-Related Institutional Role

Beyond the McKinnon case, ONI appears in UAP discourse as a suspected custodian of recovered or reverse-engineered UAP material and intelligence. The naval nexus in UAP encounters is longstanding: the Nimitz/USS Theodore Roosevelt encounters (2004, 2014–2015) were Navy incidents; the UAP office predecessors (AATIP, AAWSAP) were organized partly within the Navy Department; and David Grusch's disclosures reference a network of programs that includes naval oversight components.

[[harold-malmgren]]'s specific warning — that ONI operates as a system from which no information exits once classified — provides a theoretical framework for why McKinnon's spreadsheet data was seized, absorbed, and never publicly surfaced: the institution was designed to be information-absorptive rather than information-releasing.
