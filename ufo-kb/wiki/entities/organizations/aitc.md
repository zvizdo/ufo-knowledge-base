# AI Trust Council (AITC)

Private advocacy and governance organization co-founded by Chris Wright, oriented toward addressing existential and safety risks from advanced artificial intelligence — particularly the scenario of runaway artificial general intelligence (AGI) that develops beyond human control before sufficient safety guardrails are in place. The AITC positions itself as a counterweight to the "move fast" culture dominant in AI development, advocating for responsible AI governance by actors who believe the stakes are literally civilizational.

**Randy Anderson connection**: [[randy-anderson]], UAP whistleblower and former contractor at Naval Air Warfare Center Weapons Division (Crane, Indiana), is an AITC member. Anderson's UAP testimony covers anomalous levitating spheres at Naval Station Crane. His AITC membership — documented in the [[sct30qijfv8]] transcript — is significant because it places a defense-contractor UAP insider within an AI governance organization, suggesting that some people with direct knowledge of anomalous technology view AGI development as a converging or related existential risk.

**Chris Wright connection**: [[chris-wright]] co-founded AITC alongside his other professional activities. Wright's involvement connects AITC to the wider network of defense, intelligence, and technology figures who appear at the intersection of UAP disclosure and AI governance in the KB.

**Convergence thesis**: Multiple KB figures — including [[eric-weinstein]] and defense insiders — have identified the convergence of AGI development and UAP disclosure as a potential inflection point. The argument: if NHI possess radically advanced AI/computational systems, disclosure and AGI development may force each other into public consciousness simultaneously. AITC's mission to slow runaway AGI development could be motivated in part by insider awareness that the reference class for "what a sufficiently advanced AI looks like" already exists in classified UAP programs.

**Organizational context**: AITC operates in the AI safety landscape alongside organizations like the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI), Anthropic, and the Center for Human-Compatible AI — but with a more private, less publicly visible profile.
