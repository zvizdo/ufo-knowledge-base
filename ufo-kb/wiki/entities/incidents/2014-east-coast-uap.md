# 2014–2015 East Coast UAP Incidents

Sustained UAP encounter series detected by the **VFA-11 Red Rippers** F/A-18 Super Hornet squadron out of NAS Oceana, Virginia Beach, during a maintenance phase between deployments (2014–2015). During this period the squadron was transitioning from the APG-73 mechanically-scanned radar to the **APG-79 actively-electronically scanned array (AESA) radar** — a significantly more precise system. Objects appeared immediately on the new system that had not been visible (or were indistinguishable from false tracks) on the old one.

## Account

### Initial Detection

Objects appeared in the large working area (a defined operations "cube") off the Virginia coast. Initial assumption by pilots: software bugs or false tracks — a known artifact of older radars. This assumption was overturned when:

1. Objects behaved unlike any false track: they were **perfectly stationary in ~120-knot winds** (category-3-hurricane conditions), without positional drift, vibration, or propulsive correction
2. Objects operated at 0.6–0.8 Mach in structured patterns (holding, circular, racetrack)
3. Speed reached 1.2 Mach — supersonic for a wingless, exhaust-less object
4. Three to seven objects appeared simultaneously in the area
5. Objects were **exclusively over water**, heading east at speed when mobile

### Triple-Sensor Corroboration

ATFLIR (Forward-Looking Infrared targeting pod) was cued to the same locations and detected anomalous energy — objects appeared as an undifferentiated bright blur rather than showing aircraft-like exhaust, suggesting **uniform surface energy emission** (see [[phenomenology-categories]] — IR masking). AIM-9X missile seekers in the same range also locked onto the contacts. Three independent sensors agreeing on physical objects at identical locations ended the false-track hypothesis.

### Visual Intercept Attempts

Pilots flew intercept passes: stepping down to 800 ft below the contact, outside a 500-ft safety bubble, looking up against the blue sky, with helmet-mounted AR cuing pointing directly at the sensor-designated location. They saw nothing. Sensors reacquired after each pass.

### Near-Miss and the Cube-in-Sphere Description

Two F/A-18s flying in section (~150 ft apart) crossed the entry waypoint to their working area. An object shot between them, passing within ~50 ft of the lead pilot's cockpit. The pilot described the object as: **a dark gray or black cube inside of a clear sphere, approximately 5–15 ft in diameter** — the first visual description of this morphology from this incident cluster. See [[cube-in-sphere]].

### Unreported Near-Misses

The initial hazard report (HAZREP) triggered a safety survey revealing **four additional near-miss incidents** had occurred in the squadron without being reported. The primary reasons: career concerns, stigma, time burden, and expectation that nothing would be done.

### Scale

Graves estimates at least 10 of ~25 squadron pilots were eyewitnesses to the objects. Other squadrons operating out of the same area also saw them; Graves learned this after the 2017 *New York Times* story — asking colleagues in a ready room produced ~50% raising their hands for "Oh yeah, the cubes and spheres."

## The GOFAST Connection

The [[2015-gofast]] video was captured during this incident cluster. Graves's close friend — the WSO who recorded the footage — subsequently told Graves that the tape shows only **one of four objects in line-abreast formation**, each ~1 mile apart. The four maintained precise relative spacing throughout, implying inter-craft digital coordination. This detail was not included in the original public release.

## Evidence

- APG-79 AESA radar tracks
- ATFLIR video (the GOFAST footage being the only publicly released example)
- AIM-9X seeker locks
- Multiple pilot eyewitness accounts
- Five formal HAZREP reports (one initial + four revealed by safety survey)

## Aftermath

[[ryan-graves]] went public, initially to get safety resolution. The case did not resolve through normal channels. He became a beacon for other pilots and eventually founded [[americans-for-safe-aerospace]]. In 2023 he testified before Congress, making this incident cluster the primary documented case for routine, ongoing UAP presence in US military airspace.

The [[2017-nyt-uap-story]] (New York Times) catalyzed widespread acknowledgment among East Coast Navy pilots that they too had seen similar objects.
