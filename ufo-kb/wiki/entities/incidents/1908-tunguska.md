# 1908 Tunguska Event

On **June 30, 1908**, an aerial explosion occurred over an uninhabited region of Siberia near the Podkamennaya Tunguska River. The blast flattened approximately **2,000 square miles of trees** but killed no humans (the area was remote). It was an **airburst** — the object exploded before reaching the ground, leaving no impact crater.

## Significance for the Taurid Stream

[[randall-carlson]] argues Tunguska is "almost certainly" a member of the **Taurid meteor stream**:

1. It occurred on **June 30** — the peak of the June/summer Taurid crossing (late June/early July). Earth crosses the Taurid stream twice each year; the June crossing is the summer torids.
2. Its **radiant point** (the direction from which it came) aligns with where a Taurid-stream object would have originated — from the direction of the sun, consistent with a June Taurid trajectory.
3. The object's characteristics (airburst, large but not crater-forming) are consistent with a **friable cometary nucleus fragment** rather than a solid iron asteroid.

Carlson uses Tunguska as the modern empirical anchor for his broader argument that the Taurid stream has historically delivered catastrophic payloads and was responsible for the Younger Dryas boundary event ~12,900 years ago.

## Local Indigenous Response

The local **Evenki people** interpreted the explosion within their existing religious framework: they believed their fire god **Agni** had descended from the sky to punish them. Carlson uses this as an illustration of how ancient peoples mythologized real cosmic events — a template for understanding ancient "sky god" narratives globally.

## Scale Reference

Carlson contrasts Tunguska with the Younger Dryas event: Tunguska was a relatively diffuse, evolved Taurid fragment hitting at a time when the stream is sparse. At the peak of the stream's earlier, denser period (12,000–20,000 years ago), impacts would have been orders of magnitude more frequent and powerful.
