# 1957 UNC Chapel Hill Gravity Research Conference

A 1957 gravity research conference held at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, funded by industrialist [[agnew-bahnson]]. The conference is one of the rare mid-20th-century institutional moments at which industrial anti-gravity research and mainstream theoretical-gravity physics shared a venue.

## Context

[[agnew-bahnson]] was simultaneously funding [[thomas-townsend-brown]]'s electrogravitics experiments and sponsoring this academic conference on gravitational physics — a dual role that placed Brown's work adjacent to (though not formally inside) the program of leading theoretical physicists. Brown attended the conference or was affiliated with the presented work; the conference is one of the few institutional moments at which his anomalous results were openly discussed alongside mainstream physics.

## The Whitten Report

[[lewis-whitten]] of [[martin-corporation]]'s anti-gravity division reported at this conference that Brown possessed:

> "A weird isotope of bismuth that exhibited anti-gravity properties."

Brown's use of bismuth as a high-K dielectric in high-voltage capacitor experiments is now understood by [[bob-maguire]] and others as **proto-topological-insulator physics** — the same materials science that [[salvatore-pais]]'s Navy patents exploit. The Whitten report is therefore a primary-source documentation point for the bismuth lineage that connects 1950s industrial anti-gravity research to 21st-century Navy patents.

## Significance

- Places Brown's electrogravitics experiments inside the formal record of academic gravity research at a moment when the topic was open
- Establishes [[martin-corporation]] (later [[lockheed-martin]]) as institutionally invested in anti-gravity research at the corporate-division level
- Provides a documented material-science specificity (bismuth) that links Brown to modern Navy patent physics

## Open Questions

- Which theoretical physicists attended? (Bryce DeWitt, John Wheeler, and others were active in this period; conference participation has been only partially documented.)
- What specifically was the "weird isotope of bismuth"? Bismuth-209 is naturally stable but radioactive; the claim may reference a particular preparation rather than a literal isotope variant.
- Did the conference materials enter the classified record after the public proceedings?
