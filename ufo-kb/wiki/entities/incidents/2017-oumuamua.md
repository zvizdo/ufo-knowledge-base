# ʻOumuamua (2017 Interstellar Object)

In October 2017, the Pan-STARRS telescope in Hawaii detected an object traveling through the solar system on a trajectory that could only be explained as interstellar — it had arrived from outside the solar system. Named ʻOumuamua (Hawaiian: "scout" or "messenger from far away"), it was the first confirmed interstellar object ever detected.

## Anomalies

The object failed to match any known category:

- **No cometary tail.** Space rocks are accelerated by outgassing (sublimating ice releasing gas — the standard propulsion model for comets). ʻOumuamua showed no cometary tail despite its anomalous acceleration, invalidating the outgassing explanation.
- **Non-gravitational acceleration.** ʻOumuamua accelerated faster than gravity alone could explain as it departed the solar system. The acceleration was inversely proportional to distance from the sun — consistent with radiation pressure acting on a thin, reflective surface (a lightsail).
- **Extreme shape.** The object exhibited a length-to-width ratio above 3:1 — a "flat pancake" geometry almost never seen in natural asteroids. Such extreme flatness is a signature of artificiality: it maximizes surface area relative to mass, exactly what a lightsail design requires.
- **No propellant plume.** No outgassing, no exhaust, no observable propulsion mechanism.

## Natural-Object Hypotheses and Their Problems

The scientific community proposed several natural explanations — all contested:

| Hypothesis | Problem |
|-----------|---------|
| Chunk of frozen nitrogen | Requires a nitrogen-ice body of a type never observed; would have required explosive loss of mass inconsistent with observations |
| Hydrogen cloud | Too diffuse to hold together at the observed density |
| Cosmic dust cloud | Cannot produce the sharp light-curve variations observed |

[[avi-loeb]]: "All the proposals that were put on the table to explain the anomalies invoked a rock of a type that we've never seen before."

## Avi Loeb's Lightsail Hypothesis

Loeb proposed in a 2018 paper (with Shmuel Bialy) that ʻOumuamua's properties are consistent with a light sail — a thin structure that uses the reflection of sunlight for propulsion. His argument:

1. The anomalous acceleration and flat shape are consistent with a lightsail of interstellar origin.
2. Nature does not produce thin, solid, highly reflective objects with a length-to-width ratio exceeding 3:1.
3. Humans have built lightsails (a 1966 NASA rocket booster was confirmed to use sunlight for propulsion — discovered when Pan-STARRS found a similarly baffling object in September 2020 that turned out to be human-made).
4. Therefore, if we can build lightsails, the inference that another civilization could also build them requires no new physics — only a builder.

The hypothesis does not require the object to be inhabited or even intentionally sent — it could be defunct technological debris ("technological trash" drifting through interstellar space).

## The 2020 Vindication Candidate

In September 2020, Pan-STARRS found another baffling object: traveling away from the sun, no cometary tail, no visible propulsion — structurally similar anomaly profile to ʻOumuamua. This object turned out to be a forgotten rocket booster from a 1966 NASA mission using solar radiation for propulsion. Loeb cites this as direct evidence that humans already build lightsail-type objects — and as partial vindication of the lightsail hypothesis applied to ʻOumuamua.

## Institutional Response

Loeb's paper received significant backlash from the astronomical community. Colleagues argued it was "extraordinary to even consider that there is another intelligent civilization out there." Loeb held his position. His 2021 book *Extraterrestrial* presented the full case to a general audience and triggered the founding of the [[galileo-project]].

## Significance for the Galileo Project

ʻOumuamua is what catalyzed Loeb's entry into UAP/NHI discourse. Before 2017 he was not engaged with the topic. His response was not to argue about UFO sightings but to build instruments that could gather better data — leading directly to the Galileo Project's observatory and its interstellar-object detection mandate (one of three research tracks).
