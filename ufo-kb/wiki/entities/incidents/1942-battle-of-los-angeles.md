# 1942 Battle of Los Angeles

The Battle of Los Angeles was a February 25, 1942 incident in which an unidentified flying object traversed the California coast and was subjected to sustained anti-aircraft (AA) fire by US military forces. Despite expending more than 1,400 artillery rounds, the craft — or crafts — appeared to be entirely unaffected and continued on its course. The event occurred less than three months after Pearl Harbor, in a state of genuine wartime alert, eliminating the most common dismissal applied to later UAP cases: peacetime misperception.

## Account

In the early hours of February 25, 1942, radar stations detected an unidentified object approaching the California coast. Air raid sirens were activated across Los Angeles County, and a blackout was imposed. Anti-aircraft batteries opened fire on the object, which was visible to thousands of civilians and military personnel.

The object — illuminated by searchlights and visible against the night sky — moved slowly and deliberately down the coast. Anti-aircraft rounds were seen to burst around it with no visible effect on its trajectory or structural integrity. The engagement lasted several hours. No craft was downed. No wreckage was recovered. No enemy aircraft origin was ever established.

**General George C. Marshall** was briefed on the event at the command level. His awareness of the incident placed UAP-type phenomena within the consciousness of senior US military leadership five years before the 1947 Roswell crash and the formal establishment of intelligence structures to manage the phenomenon.

The Secretary of the Navy initially attributed the incident to "war nerves." The Secretary of War contradicted this, stating that "real objects" had been present. The contradiction was never officially resolved.

## Witnesses

- **General George C. Marshall** — Army Chief of Staff; received a command-level briefing on the incident; his awareness connects the event to the highest levels of the US military establishment.
- **Military anti-aircraft crews** — Multiple battery crews across the Los Angeles coastal defense network engaged the object directly; their operational reports constitute the primary military record.
- **Civilian population of Los Angeles** — An estimated one million civilians were awake during the blackout and observed the searchlights and AA fire; multiple photographs were taken, including the widely reproduced Los Angeles Times image showing the craft illuminated by converging searchlights.
- **Radar operators** — Tracked the object's approach and movement; instrumented observation.

## Evidence

- **Anti-aircraft engagement records** — US Army logs documenting the expenditure of 1,400+ rounds of anti-aircraft ammunition against a target that was not downed and not identified.
- **Radar tracking data** — Initial detection and tracking records from coastal radar stations.
- **Photographic evidence** — Multiple photographs, including the Los Angeles Times image; these have been subject to debate over whether they show a craft or searchlight convergence artifact, but they were taken under conditions of genuine military alert.
- **Contradictory official statements** — The public contradiction between the Secretary of the Navy ("war nerves") and the Secretary of War ("real objects") is itself evidentiary: a wartime administration did not speak with one voice because the internal facts were not settled.
- **No wreckage or biological material** — Unlike Roswell, no physical recovery followed; the craft was not downed.

The multi-witness, instrumented (radar and AA fire), and photographic character of the evidence places this among the most robustly documented early UAP incidents.

## Aftermath

- No official explanation satisfying both the radar tracks and the visual reports was ever issued.
- The event preceded by five years the institutional structures (CIA, MJ-12, Project Sign) that would later manage the UAP issue — meaning it occurred in a bureaucratic vacuum, with awareness concentrated at the command level (Marshall) without a dedicated management architecture.
- Marshall's briefing on the event may represent the earliest documented instance of a US Chief of Staff being made aware of a UAP military engagement.
