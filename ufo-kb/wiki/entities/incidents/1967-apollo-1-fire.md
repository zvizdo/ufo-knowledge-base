# Apollo 1 Fire (1967)

Launch pad test accident on January 27, 1967 that killed all three Apollo 1 crew members — [[gus-grissom]], Ed White, and Roger Chaffee — in a flash fire inside the Command Module during a routine test. The official finding: faulty wiring in the capsule ignited in the pure-oxygen atmosphere.

## Account

At approximately 6:31 PM EST, a fire started inside the Command Module at Launch Complex 34. The pure-oxygen atmosphere at high pressure (higher than sea-level) created conditions where fire spread extremely rapidly. The hatch, which opened inward and required several steps to open, could not be opened in time. All three crew members died of asphyxiation within 17 seconds of the fire starting.

## Suspicious Context

[[gus-grissom]]'s death is particularly significant in the moon-landing skepticism literature because of two documented facts:

1. **Lemon protest**: Grissom had publicly hung a lemon on a coat hanger outside the capsule at a press conference, calling the vehicle "a lemon, a piece of junk," after complaints about the capsule's defects went unaddressed. He was the most publicly critical NASA astronaut of program management at the time.

2. **CIA on the launch pad**: The night before the fire — January 26, 1967 — Grissom told his wife: *"For some strange reason, the CIA is all over the launch pad today. I wonder why they're here inspecting the equipment. I've never seen them here before."*

Both facts are documented historical record. Their combination — public criticism + CIA presence + death the next day — is the core of the argument that Grissom was deliberately killed.

## Significance

If the moon landing was staged (see [[moon-landing-hoax]]), Grissom's position as the most likely first man on the Moon (as Apollo 1 commander he would have commanded Apollo 11 or equivalent) and his willingness to publicly criticize the program made him both the most visible obstacle and the most dangerous potential whistleblower. His death removed both problems simultaneously.

The use of a pure-oxygen atmosphere at high pressure — a known fire risk that engineers had flagged — is also cited as either negligence or deliberate vulnerability creation.
