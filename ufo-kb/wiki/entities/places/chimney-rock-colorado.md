# Chimney Rock (Colorado)

A Chacoan outlier site in southwestern Colorado, now a national monument. Two massive natural stone pinnacles rise ~1,000 feet above the valley floor, connected by a ridge. On that ridge, at the right location within a circular great kiva, an observer can peer between the two pinnacles and see the moon rise — but only at a specific astronomical moment.

## The 18.6-Year Lunar Standstill

[[randall-carlson]] describes Chimney Rock as a deliberately positioned observatory for the **18.6-year lunar standstill cycle** (also called the major lunar standstill or lunistice). The Moon's rising and setting points oscillate between a maximum and minimum over 18.6 years. Every 18.6 years, the Moon rises in exact alignment with the gap between Chimney Rock's twin pinnacles as seen from the kiva.

The Chacoan people — who built a ~10,000-square-mile civilization centered on Chaco Canyon in the San Juan Basin of New Mexico — constructed this outlier precisely for this observation. Chimney Rock represents one of several outlier sites connected to Chaco Canyon via roads and line-of-sight communication.

Carlson planned a visit to Chimney Rock to witness the upcoming 18.6-year standstill event, which the park service was expected to open for public hiking to the ridge.

## Significance for Ancient Astronomy

The site demonstrates that ancestral Puebloan people:
- Understood and tracked the 18.6-year lunar cycle with precision
- Built permanent architectural observatories aligned to celestial events on multi-decadal timescales
- Used elevated outlier sites as part of a regional communication and ceremonial network

Carlson frames this in a broader argument that ancient peoples across the world paid "obsessive attention" to the Moon — building enormous structures to track its subtle cycles — for reasons that modern people, who rarely observe the sky, no longer understand.
