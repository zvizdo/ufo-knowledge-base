# Tic-Tac

The "Tic-Tac" is the informal name for a white, oblong, featureless craft observed and recorded by F/A-18 Super Hornet pilots from the USS Nimitz Carrier Strike Group in November 2004 off the coast of San Diego, California. The FLIR1 video of the encounter — captured by the aircraft's forward-looking infrared system — was released publicly in 2017 and was officially acknowledged by the Pentagon in 2020 as authentic Department of Defense footage.

## Description

The craft was described by pilots (most notably Commander David Fravor and Lt. Commander Alex Dietrich) as:
- White, approximately 40 feet long, oblong — shaped like a Tic-Tac breath mint
- No visible wings, rotors, exhaust plume, or propulsion system
- Capable of hovering in place against winds and then accelerating to hypersonic speeds instantaneously
- Exhibiting apparent awareness of and response to the approaching F/A-18s
- Capable of descending from approximately 60,000 feet to near sea level in seconds and then reversing

The observed flight characteristics match [[gravity-manipulation-propulsion]] as described by [[lue-elizondo]]: no conventional propulsion signature, extreme acceleration and deceleration without structural stress effects, and apparent metric-warping behavior.

## Significance

The Tic-Tac encounter is the empirical anchor of the modern UAP disclosure argument. It is:
- **Radar-corroborated**: tracked by the USS Princeton's SPY-1B radar for two weeks prior
- **Multi-witness**: multiple pilots observed the craft directly
- **Instrumented**: captured on FLIR video
- **Pentagon-acknowledged**: DoD officially confirmed the video's authenticity

[[aatip]] under [[lue-elizondo]] used this encounter as a paradigm case. The craft's performance profile is inconsistent with any known human aerospace technology, which forms the core of the [[credibility-frameworks]] argument in the post-2017 disclosure context.
